# 12.10.2026 - The payload gimbal, the scenery, the flight log, and a
# reorganised console

Asked for, in one message: a screen for the gimbal camera with zoom, IR,
laser rangefinder, video and photo; drag to slew and wheel to zoom;
target selection that holds the gimbal on a point; natural and man-made
features in every environment including a north European one; most of
the left column moved into categorised settings; the menu icon moved to
the far right; the navigation controls rearranged sensibly on both
columns; camera selection always within reach, DJI-style; aircraft
movement as realistic as possible; at least four chase-view angles; a
deep research pass against authoritative sources; and flight log
recording with a performance log.

The research is in `docs/simulator-practice-research.md`, including what
it could NOT establish. This entry is the decisions.

## The gimbal is real, not a picture of one

The previous session hung three static shapes under the belly so the
aircraft LOOKED like the photographs. `tools/gimbal_apply.py` replaces
them with two revolute joints, a camera and a one-ray lidar, and removes
the old shapes so it does not matter which order the tools are run in.

**Driven straight over gz-transport, not through PX4.** PX4 v1.15 does
have a gimbal manager, and going that way would have meant giving PX4 a
gimbal device, setting `MNT_MODE_IN`/`MNT_MODE_OUT`, and routing every
command console → MAVLink → PX4 → gimbal. The station already talks to
Gazebo directly for the lights and the cameras. The cost is real and is
written in the code: the gimbal is not in a PX4 log and PX4 does not
stabilise it.

**The controller gains are arithmetic, not taste.** The first draft used
`p_gain = 2.2`, copied from nothing in particular. Against the ball's
inertia of 3e-05 kg m² that is an angular acceleration of a hundred
thousand radians a second squared - the gimbal would have slammed
between its stops every timestep. The gains now come from
`P = I ωn²` and `D = 2 I ωn` at ωn = 12 rad/s, and the inertia figures
are written out in the model file above them so the next person can
check the arithmetic.

**The signs are the part that silently fails.** A camera looks along its
own +X; the model's body frame is FLU, so a positive yaw joint turns it
to PORT while an operator means starboard when they say "pan right".
That is one sign flip, it does not crash, and it would have produced a
console that pans the wrong way for ever. It is in the module docstring,
in the model file, and in four checks in `tools/test_payload.py`.

## Stabilisation, and why the first version was wrong

The first draft left the mount unstabilised in manual pointing and said
so honestly on screen: "the mount is bolted to the airframe". That is an
accurate description of the model, and it is still the wrong thing to
ship. Every real EO/IR ball is stabilised; a student taught on a picture
that rolls with the aircraft is being taught a defect.

So the station stabilises it instead. A manual aim is remembered as a
bearing and a depression **in the world**, and the joint angles are
recomputed from the aircraft's own attitude ten times a second. Target
lock is the stronger version of the same thing: the aim comes from a
fixed ground point, so it survives the aircraft moving as well as
turning. It can be switched off, which shows what the bare mount does -
worth seeing once.

A detail that took a second attempt: the holding loop must not overwrite
the remembered world direction with its own corrections, or the gimbal
follows the aircraft round one small step at a time. Hence the
`remember` argument on `aim()`.

## Range: measured, computed, and saying which

The lidar gives a real range when the ray hits something. When it does
not - no gimbal in the model yet, or the ray went into the sky - the
station intersects the line of sight with the ground at the aircraft's
own height and **says that is what it did**. The console shows the
source and the age of every reading. That follows the one piece of EO/IR
integration guidance found that was worth following: a bare number
cannot be told apart from a stale one.

## Zoom is digital, and labelled digital

No documented way to change a camera's field of view at runtime in
gz-sim 8 was found. So the middle of the frame is cropped and scaled
back up, which is what a real payload's digital zoom does. The reticle
says "digital zoom" on it. An optical zoom is not modelled and is not
claimed.

## Target lock runs slew → settle → range → fix

Not range-then-slew. Ranging before the slew measures whatever happened
to be under the boresight, which is not what the operator clicked on.
This is also the order a real slew-to-cue-then-lase sequence runs in.

## Four chase angles from one camera

Four more cameras would be four more things to render on a machine whose
graphics were already called marginal in July. Instead the one chase
camera moved onto a two-axis boom:
behind / left / right / front / top / low are six positions of one
camera. The station subscribes to the old base_link topic and the new
boom topic at the same time, so `tools/chase_angles_apply.py` stays
optional and a machine that has not run it keeps the fixed view.

## The console: navigation on the rails, everything else behind the menu

The old split was by audience - pilot left, instructor right - and it
had put weather, lighting, world loading and camera plumbing in a column
the pilot's eye travels down while flying.

Now the rails are the sortie, in the order it is flown: Launch, In
flight, Recover on the left; Flight mode, Navigation setup, Emergency on
the right. The rails are driven by a list of plugin IDs rather than by
category, because the ORDER is part of the teaching and the categories
do not carry one.

Everything set once lives in the settings window, which now has
categories down the side. The menu icon is the last thing on the top bar.

**`unplaced_controls()` exists so this cannot go wrong quietly.** Any
plugin that no rail, window or settings group claims is swept into the
settings window's last group, and a browser check fails the build if any
control is unreachable. A control that vanishes in a reshuffle looks
exactly like a control that moved.

### Where the control strips went, and the bug that decided it

The payload and chase strips started across the bottom of the picture,
which is where DJI puts them. The bottom of this screen is full: the
attitude instrument is centred on the band and the two corner views sit
either side of it. The strip had to overlap one of the three, and being
inside the hero's stacking context it could not rise above a corner
view - so it sat underneath one and swallowed its own clicks. The
browser test caught it as a 30-second timeout rather than as anything
that looked like a layout fault. They are at the top of the picture now,
where there is nothing.

The top bar also had to tighten below 1300 px: a fourth view tab and a
Log button pushed it over the aircraft's state at 1100. The badge goes
and the padding shrinks. The browser check measures it and fails the
build.

## The flight log

Automatic, from ARM to DISARM, because a log an instructor has to
remember to start is a log with holes in it. A half-second track as CSV -
which opens in Excel, which is what an instructor actually has - and a
summary beside it.

The figure worth calling out is `limit_breaches`: not whether a limit
was touched but **how many seconds were spent outside it and how far**.
A one-sample spike and a two-minute excursion are not the same event and
should not read the same in a record.

A disarm shorter than 20 s does not end a flight, so a bounce on landing
or a re-arm on the pad is one circuit rather than three. A flight that
never leaves the ground is still recorded: an arm, a run-up and a disarm
is a real event in a training record.

No student identity is invented. `event_logger.py` already explains why
that is the Head of Training's decision and not this file's.

## Scenery, and a new place to fly

Every world was bare. That was survivable when the pictures were a nose
camera at the horizon and a chase camera at the aircraft. With a gimbal
it is not: a sensor operator cannot be taught to find and hold a target
in an empty desert.

`scenery.py` holds what is in each place and why those things - boulders
and a wadi in the Hoggar, sage and a hangar on the playa, saltbush and a
telegraph line on the Nullarbor, harvest mounds and a rail line at
Uyuni.

**`training_area_lowlands` is new**: the Cambridgeshire fens. The other
four areas are all pale and empty, which is one lesson repeated. This is
the clutter a sensor operator actually works against - hedged fields,
woodland, a farm, a church, a turbine, pylons. Flat, for the same reason
the others were. Its magnetic field is computed with IGRF for its own
coordinates, the way `tools/fix_magnetic_field.py` does the rest.

Decisions inside it:

* **Primitives only.** A mesh is a file that has to be installed on the
  teaching machine and found by Gazebo's resource path. A box with a
  pitched roof is obviously a building from 120 m up, which is the only
  range anyone sees it from.
* **Buildings and masts collide; vegetation does not.** An obstacle an
  aircraft can fly through teaches a student that obstacles do not
  matter. A tree with collision at 25 m/s is a crash with no lesson in it
  and a lot of contact solving.
* **90 m clear round the launch point.** That is where the aircraft
  spawns, arms, hovers and lands.
* **Deterministic layout.** A lesson that says "the orange container
  north-east of the hangar" has to still be true next week, so the layout
  comes from the world's own name through a fixed generator - not
  `random`, whose stream is not guaranteed stable across Python versions.
* **Three numbered chequer targets in every area, at the same bearings
  and ranges.** A student moving from the desert to the fens should be
  reading a different landscape, not learning three new positions.
  Target 3 at 700 m is deliberately outside the 500 m range limit: it can
  be identified from inside the limit, and flying out to it will show in
  the flight log.

## "As realistic as possible", answered precisely

The flight dynamics are PX4 SITL's own against Gazebo's LiftDrag and the
airframe's own coefficients. That IS the reference implementation.
Hand-tuning it would make it worse, and this project did not touch it.
The IMU already carries PX4's own noise and bias figures.

What was unrealistic was the air: every world shipped with its wind at
exactly zero and nothing set it unless someone pressed a button. An
aircraft hovering in mathematically still air sits as though nailed
down.

`plugins/conditions.py` sets the air by Beaufort force - the standard
scale, so "Force 4" means what it means in a forecast - and the station
applies Force 1 twenty-five seconds after start-up, by which time Gazebo
is usually listening. One metre a second is barely perceptible and not
zero. Press Calm to switch it off. The world files already carried the
sinusoidal and Gaussian variation, so the mean breathes rather than
pushes.

## What has NOT been done

Honestly, and this is the long list:

* **Nothing Gazebo-side has been watched running.** Not the gimbal, not
  the rangefinder, not the chase boom, not the scenery, not the new
  world, not the wind, not the lights, not the aircraft recolour, not
  the headless start. All of it parses, all of it is arithmetic off the
  model's own figures, and none of it has been seen.
* **The payload camera is a third rendered camera** on a machine whose
  graphics were called marginal in July, and the worlds now have 40-130
  more models in them. Nobody has measured what that costs in frame rate.
* **The 0.22 m lower stance from the landing legs** has not been watched
  at spawn.
* **The CAP 722B clause numbers are still unverified** against the
  printed fifth edition. Somebody with the PDF needs to check them.
* **The wind direction convention** is still reasoned rather than
  measured - `plugins/wind_gust.py` has said so since 11 Oct. One flight
  settles it: wind from the west, aircraft drifts east.
* **Two-crew operation** - a separate pilot and sensor operator at
  separate stations, which is how the role is split in the real world -
  is not built. The payload is a tab on one console.

## The checks

Three new ones, all in CLAUDE.md's before-committing list:
`tools/test_payload.py` (the pointing geometry against hand-worked cases,
the model edit, and the whole payload screen in a real browser),
`tools/test_flight_log.py` (a made-up sortie whose every number is known
in advance), `tools/test_scenery.py` (the launch point stays clear, the
layout is reproducible, the targets are where the notes say, and the
notes are regenerated from the same source the worlds are).
