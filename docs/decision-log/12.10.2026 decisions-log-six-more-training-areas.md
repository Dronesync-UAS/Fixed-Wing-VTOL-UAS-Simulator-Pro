# 12.10.2026 - Six more training areas, and a way to fetch real ground

Asked for after the scenery work: "do you have some realistic
environments to add?" - then "give all scripts".

## What was added

| Key | Where | What it teaches that nothing else did |
|---|---|---|
| `coast` | North Norfolk | A shoreline. The best navigation feature a pilot gets, and the first time a student flies with no vertical references over water. |
| `airfield` | Flat eastern England | Circuits against real runway markings - a threshold to aim at and a centreline to judge drift from. |
| `estate` | Thames estuary | Clutter and obstacles. Everything is a grey rectangle, and there is a 52 m chimney and two 32 m cranes with collision. |
| `forest` | Thetford, Breckland | A target can be lost under canopy. The firebreak grid is the only thing that makes a plantation navigable. |
| `valley` | North Wales foothills | The horizon is 200 m above you, line of sight goes behind a spur, and the gimbal has to look UP. |
| `flood` | Somerset Levels | The UAS job that actually pays. Water and ground are nearly the same colour and the edge is genuinely hard to find. |

Each is a real place with real coordinates and a real magnetic field,
computed with IGRF via `ppigrf` for 2026-10-12 - the same way
`tools/fix_magnetic_field.py` did the older four. The airfield is the
exception and says so: a flat spot in eastern England, with a generic
layout that is not a survey of any real aerodrome.

## The decisions inside that

### Flat ground plus scenery, not heightmaps

Five of the six are a plane with scenery over it. That is not laziness:
the Fens, the Levels, an airfield and a Breckland plantation genuinely
are flat, a heightmap of flat ground is megabytes of nothing, and the
station caps the ceiling at 120 m - so relief inside the 500 m range
limit leaves no airspace a student can legally fly in. That is the
mistake the Hoggar world made and `tools/widen_clearing.py` had to fix
afterwards.

### The valley is the exception, and its terrain is made up

A flat valley is not a valley. So `tools/make_valley_heightmap.py`
generates one: dead flat out to 520 m either side of the axis - beyond
the range limit, so there is still no relief anywhere a student can
legally fly - rising to 240 m by 1400 m out.

**It is synthetic and says so in three places.** The coordinates and the
magnetic field are real because PX4's EKF needs a field that matches the
latitude it is told it is at. The shape of the ground is not a survey of
anywhere and must not be described to a student as one.

Three sine terms of different wavelengths give the ridge spurs and cols.
They are normalised by the most they can sum to, so the tallest point is
exactly 240 m and nothing clips at pixel 255 - a clipped ridge comes out
as a flat table top, which is the one shape a real ridge never has.

### One model per wood, not one per tree

The first version of the forest was 725 static models, because every
conifer was its own. Gazebo pays per model. Grouping each plantation
block into one model with two dozen shapes in it took it to 101 models,
which is what a world with a hundred buildings costs. The same change
was made to the fens' woodland blocks and the coast's pine belt.

### Water is a flat plate, and the flood water is not blue

Nothing floats, there are no waves, and an aircraft that ditches will
sit on top of it. It is there to be a different colour, to be a
different thing in infrared, and to be navigated by.

Flood water is a dull brown-green, not sea blue, because flood water
carries soil - and because being nearly the same colour as the field
underneath it is the whole lesson in that area.

### A bug worth recording

`valley()` passed a bare `box(...)` where the writer wanted a list of
shapes. A shape is a two-tuple, so it unpacked one level too shallow and
failed as "too many values to unpack" inside the XML writer, about two
hundred lines from the line that caused it. `tools/test_scenery.py` now
walks every part of every theme and checks its shape before any of it
reaches the writer.

Also: `tools/test_scenery.py` counted a 7 x 6 x 3.2 m outbuilding on the
coast as a fourth payload target, because it matched the panel on two
dimensions out of three. The check now includes the thickness.

## Real terrain, for when it is actually needed

`tools/fetch_terrain.py` downloads real elevation for any world's own
coordinates and writes a heightmap beside the others.

**Not run automatically, and not run by default.** Two reasons, both
worth stating: everything else in this station works offline after
installation, and the good global source needs a free API key -
downloading several hundred megabytes on somebody's behalf without
asking is not acceptable.

Two sources, both named in the output so it is always clear what was
used and under what terms:

* **OpenTopography** (default) - Copernicus GLO-30, 30 m postings,
  global, free for non-commercial use, needs a free key.
* **AWS Terrain Tiles** (`--provider terrarium`) - open data, no key, no
  registration, global. Elevation encoded in the pixel as
  `(R*256 + G + B/256) - 32768` metres.

**The important thing it does is refuse politely.** Before it writes
anything it measures the relief within 500 m of the launch point and
warns, in the station's own terms, if there is no airspace left. It then
prints the exact `<heightmap>` block to paste into the world file rather
than editing it silently - because swapping a world's ground is a change
somebody should make on purpose.

`tools/test_terrain_fetch.py` checks the tile numbering against values
anyone can verify by hand, the Terrarium encoding against its published
formula, the relief measurement against a grid whose answer is known,
and that every world's coordinates can be read back out of its own file.
**The download path itself has never executed** - neither host is
reachable from the machine this was written on.

## What has NOT been done

* **None of the six has been loaded in Gazebo.** Not one. They parse as
  XML, their scenery is checked by `tools/test_scenery.py`, and nobody
  has looked at any of them.
* **No aircraft has flown in any of them**, and the valley in particular
  has an assumption in it that only a flight can settle: that the floor
  really is flat enough for a PX4 VTOL to sit still on. The Hoggar world
  needed a resting-drift fix for exactly that.
* **The frame-rate cost is unmeasured.** The estate is 125 models and
  the forest is 101 models holding 1300 shapes, on a machine whose
  graphics were called marginal in July.
* **The forest's trees have no collision**, so an aircraft flies through
  them. That is the project-wide rule and it is right, but it does mean
  that area cannot teach obstacle avoidance among the trees. The estate
  can - its chimney and cranes do collide.
* **The airfield's markings are proportioned, not surveyed.** A dashed
  centreline and threshold bars in the usual proportions; not a
  reproduction of any published aerodrome chart.
